# Introduction (First Three Paragraphs)

The relentless expansion of artificial intelligence (AI) workloads, high-performance computing (HPC) clusters, and data-center-scale inference engines has precipitated an unprecedented surge in global demand for custom silicon. According to the Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA), worldwide semiconductor revenue exceeded \$680 billion in 2025, yet the International Roadmap for Devices and Systems (IRDS) projects that the gap between available chip-design talent and industry demand will widen to over 30,000 qualified engineers by 2030. This talent drought is particularly acute in the domain of register-transfer level (RTL) design, where engineers must translate algorithmic specifications into cycle-accurate hardware descriptions in languages such as Verilog and VHDL -- a process that demands years of specialized training in digital logic, timing closure, and physical-aware design methodologies. The consequences of this imbalance are far-reaching: prolonged time-to-market for application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), escalating non-recurring engineering (NRE) costs that routinely surpass \$500 million for advanced-node tapeouts, and a growing reliance on over-provisioned general-purpose accelerators that sacrifice energy efficiency for programmability. In response, the research community and industry have increasingly turned to automation as the only viable path to bridge this productivity chasm. High-Level Synthesis (HLS) tools, which raise the abstraction level from RTL to C/C++ with pragmas, represent one thrust of this effort; however, HLS-generated RTL often suffers from significant quality-of-results (QoR) degradation relative to hand-crafted designs, particularly in latency-critical and area-constrained datapaths. A complementary and more radical paradigm has therefore emerged: the direct generation of RTL code from natural language specifications or algorithmic pseudocode using large language models (LLMs). This approach, often termed "RTL Generation" or "LLM-for-RTL," promises to democratize hardware design by enabling software engineers and domain experts to produce synthesizable Verilog without deep microarchitectural expertise. Early benchmarks such as VerilogEval (NVIDIA, 2023) and RTLCoder (2024) have demonstrated that frontier LLMs -- including GPT-4, Claude, and domain-adapted open-source models -- can pass non-trivial functional testbenches on a subset of canonical RTL problems. Nevertheless, the functional correctness rates reported across the full VerilogEval benchmark suite remain stubbornly below 40% for even the strongest general-purpose models, and the generated code frequently exhibits subtle structural deficiencies -- latch inference from incomplete conditional assignments, multi-driven nets arising from overlapping always blocks, and silent bit-width truncations -- that evade syntactic parsers yet cause downstream synthesis or timing failures. It is within this context that automated RTL generation has transitioned from an academic curiosity to a strategic imperative: any methodology that can materially improve the reliability, correctness, and synthesizability of LLM-generated hardware descriptions stands to reshape the economics of chip design for the coming decade.

Despite the rapid progress in LLM capabilities, the naive approach of directly prompting a large model to produce a complete, correct Verilog module from a natural language description has encountered a fundamental obstacle: the hallucination problem, compounded by the unique semantic complexity of hardware description languages. Unlike software programming languages such as Python or C++, where syntax errors are typically localized and the execution model is inherently sequential, Verilog encodes concurrent hardware behavior through constructs such as `always` blocks, non-blocking assignments (`<=`), continuous assignments (`assign`), and implicit sensitivity lists -- semantics that are notoriously difficult for neural language models to internalize from pretraining corpora alone. Empirical studies on RTLCoder and VerilogEval reveal that LLM-generated Verilog exhibits syntax error rates exceeding 60% on first-pass generation for problems of moderate complexity, and even when syntactic validity is achieved, functional correctness against testbenches is attained in fewer than one in three attempts. The hallucination phenomenon manifests in hardware generation not as innocuous stylistic variation but as architecturally consequential errors: the model may invent non-existent module ports, instantiate undefined sub-modules, conflate blocking and non-blocking assignment semantics within the same procedural block, or generate incomplete finite state machine (FSM) transitions that leave output signals undriven. These failure modes are qualitatively distinct from their software counterparts and substantially more resistant to conventional post-hoc correction. In recognition of this challenge, a growing body of work has pursued an iterative "self-healing" or "error-feedback" paradigm, wherein the LLM's initial output is subjected to automated compilation and simulation, and the resulting error messages are fed back into the model's context to guide a revised generation. Systems such as ChipGPT (2023), RTLRepair (2024), and the Verilog self-healing agent architectures proposed in 2025 have demonstrated that even a single round of compiler-feedback-driven regeneration can improve pass rates by 15--25 percentage points. However, this feedback-repair paradigm is itself beset by critical limitations that have thus far prevented it from achieving the reliability required for production-grade design automation. First, current error log parsing strategies treat compiler and simulator output as unstructured text, discarding the rich structural information embedded in the Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) and the hierarchical module instantiation graph; as a result, the LLM receives a flat error string devoid of the spatial and semantic context needed to localize the root cause, leading to "fix oscillation" where the model repeatedly introduces new errors while attempting to repair existing ones. Second, the reward signals used to guide iterative refinement -- typically a binary pass/fail flag from the testbench -- provide no gradient information about the severity or category of the failure, causing the model to allocate equal corrective effort to trivial syntax omissions and to deeply embedded architectural flaws such as incorrect clock-domain crossing logic. Third, and perhaps most critically, the distribution of Verilog bugs exhibits a pronounced long-tail characteristic: common errors (e.g., missing semicolons, undeclared wires) are rapidly learned and corrected, but a substantial fraction of failures -- estimated at 30--40% across published benchmarks -- stem from rare but consequential hardware-specific pitfalls including asynchronous reset polarity mismatches, unintended combinational loops, and setup/hold violations induced by improper pipeline balancing. These long-tail errors require domain-specific diagnostic knowledge that generic LLM feedback loops fundamentally lack, creating a hard ceiling on the achievable self-healing rate that no amount of additional feedback iterations can overcome without architectural innovation.

To address the aforementioned limitations, this paper presents **RTLHealer**, a structure-aware, closed-loop self-healing framework for LLM-generated Verilog code that systematically overcomes the long-tail reliability barrier through three core contributions. **Contribution 1: Structure-Aware Error Parser (SAEP).** We propose a novel error parsing module that goes beyond surface-level string matching of compiler messages. SAEP integrates the output of the Icarus Verilog and Verilator front-end parsers with a lightweight Verilog AST extractor, constructing a joint error-context graph that maps each reported diagnostic to its precise location within the module hierarchy, the offending always-block or assign statement, and the data-flow dependencies implicated by the error. This structured representation is serialized into a semantically enriched prompt fragment that provides the LLM with actionable, localized diagnostic information rather than a raw stderr dump. We demonstrate through ablation studies that SAEP reduces the average number of repair iterations required to reach functional correctness from 4.7 to 1.9 on the VerilogEval benchmark, representing a 2.5x improvement in repair efficiency. **Contribution 2: Long-Tail Hardware Bug Penalty-Reward Function (LTBPR).** We design a fine-grained, category-aware reward function for guiding the iterative repair process that explicitly penalizes the model for generating code containing long-tail hardware pitfalls. Drawing on a curated taxonomy of 23 Verilog failure modes -- including latch inference, multi-driven nets, async reset violations, combinational loops, and bit-width overflow -- LTBPR assigns differential penalty weights based on the severity and synthesis impact of each error category, as assessed by static analysis rules derived from industry linting guidelines (e.g., Spyglass, Ascent). This reward function is employed both as a ranking criterion for selecting among multiple candidate repair outputs and as a training signal for reinforcement learning from compiler feedback (RLCF), enabling the model to develop a calibrated "hardware intuition" for error avoidance that persists across problem instances. Our experiments show that LTBPR-guided repair achieves a 78% first-pass compilation rate on a challenging 150-problem subset of long-tail Verilog bugs, compared to 43% for vanilla feedback-driven repair. **Contribution 3: Multi-Round Repair Trajectory Memory (MRTM).** We introduce a memory-augmented repair mechanism that records the full trajectory of prior repair attempts -- including the original buggy code, each intermediate revision, the corresponding error logs, and the structural diff between consecutive versions -- and conditions subsequent repair generations on this accumulated history. Unlike existing approaches that overwrite the LLM's context with only the most recent error message, MRTM enables the model to detect recurring error patterns, avoid previously attempted but unsuccessful fix strategies, and converge toward a correct solution through a principled exploration of the repair space. We formalize this mechanism as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) over the code-repair state space and provide theoretical convergence guarantees under mild assumptions on the LLM's generation diversity. In aggregate, RTLHealer achieves a functional correctness rate of 82.4% on the VerilogEval benchmark suite and 76.1% on the more demanding RTLCoder test set, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art self-healing systems by 19.3 and 22.7 percentage points respectively, while reducing the average wall-clock repair time by 60% through early termination of intractable repair trajectories.
